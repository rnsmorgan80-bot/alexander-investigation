THE HISTORICAL PROBLEM

Death is certain.
The story is not.

Contemporary Babylonian scholars recorded Alexander's death without explaining its cause. Greek and Roman authors wrote elaborate narratives centuries later. Some remembered fever and failing strength; others imagined an extraordinary plot.

The problem is not simply to ask who had a motive? Many powerful men gained from Alexander's death. The historian must ask whether an account is early, independent, plausible and corroborated — and whether a dramatic ending tells us more about the storyteller than the event.

Historical method: Benefiting from Alexander’s death is not evidence of causing it. The investigation is a fictional device built around genuine ancient source traditions.

⚠ THE TWIST: NOT ALL WITNESSES WERE THERE.

The Babylonian tablet was written close to the events. Plutarch and Arrian wrote roughly four centuries later, Diodorus around three centuries later, and versions of the Alexander Romance later still. An impressive story is not eyewitness testimony. The alleged Royal Journals do not survive independently.

New question: Could a dramatic accusation tell us more about later struggles for legitimacy than about Alexander’s final days?

01Cuneiform record

A Babylonian scholar

Astronomical Diary (BM 45962) · 323 BCE · contemporary
What this account can tell us

The death was recorded by temple scholars close to the event. It does not name a disease, a poison or a suspect.

Read the Babylonian sign transliteration passage and English translation
[…] NIM GUB 29 LUGAL NAM.MEŠ DIR AN […]
Translation

[…] The twenty-ninth: the king died. Clouds. […]

The sign-level transcription is excerpted and damaged; this is not a continuous reconstructed Akkadian sentence.

Investigate: Does an absence of explanation make a conspiracy more likely?

02Moral biography

Plutarch

Life of Alexander 75.3 · Early 2nd century CE
What this account can tell us

Plutarch rejects the sensational suggestion of a sudden pain as though Alexander had been struck by a spear.

Read the Greek passage and English translation
Μηδίου δεηθέντος ᾤχετο κωμασόμενος πρὸς αὐτόν κἀκεῖ πιὼν ὅλην τὴν ἐπιοῦσαν ἡμέραν ἤρξατο πυρέττειν
Translation

At Medius’ invitation he went to join the drinking party, and after drinking on the following day began to suffer fever.

Excerpt and punctuation adapted for display; text and translation from the corrected seminar anthology.

Investigate: When a biographer criticises other versions as theatrical, should we believe him?

03Campaign history

Arrian

Anabasis 7.25–26 · 2nd century CE
What this account can tell us

Arrian refers to lost court day-books and presents a chronology of a prolonged illness.

Read the Greek passage and English translation
Καὶ αἱ βασίλειοι ἐφημερίδες ὧδε ἔχουσιν· πίνειν παρὰ Μηδίῳ αὐτὸν κωμάσαντα
Translation

And the Royal Journals give this account: that he drank after attending a revel at Medius’ house.

This extract is part of a longer illness narrative.

Investigate: If Arrian and Plutarch used related material, are they truly two independent witnesses?

04Universal history

Diodorus Siculus

Library of History 17.117 · 1st century BCE
What this account can tell us

Diodorus preserves a strikingly dramatic account unlike the gradual-fever presentation.

Read the Greek passage and English translation
ἄφνω δὲ ὥσπερ ὑπό τινος πληγῆς ἰσχυρᾶς πεπληγμένος ἀνεστέναξε μέγα βοήσας
Translation

Suddenly, as if struck by a powerful blow, he groaned and gave a loud cry.

The excerpt describes a literary report, not independently observed symptoms.

Investigate: Why might a sudden terrible cry make a better story than an illness lasting days?

05Rhetorical Roman history

Curtius Rufus

History of Alexander 10.5 · Probably 1st century CE
What this account can tell us

Curtius stages the disputed succession, not an eyewitness statement about the cause of death.

Read the Latin passage and English translation
detractum anulum digito Perdiccae tradidit … Quaerentibusque his, cui relinqueret regnum, respondit ei, qui esset optimus.
Translation

He took the ring from his finger and gave it to Perdiccas. … Asked to whom he left the kingdom, he replied, “To whoever was best.”

His precise lifetime is uncertain. The ellipsis marks omitted text.

Investigate: Is “to the best” the same as the Greek tradition “to the strongest”?

06Historical romance

The Alexander Romance

Pseudo-Callisthenes, Greek α recension, Book 3 · Compiled centuries after 323 BCE
What this account can tell us

This is evidence of an increasingly elaborate later legend, not independent verification of a murder.

Read the Greek passage and English translation
μεταπεμψάμενος οὖν [τὸ] τοξικὸν φάρμακον … καὶ ἐμβαλὼν εἰς ἡμιόνου χηλὴν ἐξεσμένην
Translation

[The conspirator] obtained a deadly poison … and placed it in a hollowed-out mule’s hoof.

The work has multiple recensions. Square brackets mark editorial supplementation; omissions are shown by ellipses.

Investigate: Why would later readers find a detailed poison plot more satisfying than an uncertain illness?

    Reveal the real historical outcome (301 BCE)

    At the Battle of Ipsus in 301 BCE, Antigonus was defeated and killed by an opposing coalition. His death strengthened the division of Alexander's former empire. This historical outcome is evidence; the sliders above are invented, illustrative counterfactuals and cannot predict what would have happened under different conditions.

    323–301 BCE: fragmentation as a process

    323 Alexander dies at Babylon. Philip III becomes king, and Roxane’s unborn child is provisionally accepted as a potential co-heir; Perdiccas controls royal administration.
    322 The Lamian War ends with Macedonian control reasserted in Greece.
    321 Perdiccas is killed during the Egyptian campaign; Triparadisus redistributes commands.
    319–317 Antipater’s death opens a new regency struggle. Philip III and Adea Eurydice are killed.
    316 Olympias is killed; Cassander dominates Macedon and confines Roxane and Alexander IV.
    311/310 Alexander IV and Roxane are killed. The legitimate Argead succession effectively ends.
    306–305 Major Successors openly assume royal titles.
    301 Antigonus dies at Ipsus. Durable successor kingdoms emerge.
